## How do I read an essay assignment prompt?
Start by reading the assignment prompt carefully on your own, even if your instructor already introduced it in class. Many students ask for help before reading the prompt, and the prompt often answers many of their questions. Some assignments are open-ended, but most ask you to do something specific, such as putting new ideas in context, analyzing course texts, or researching something related to the course. Also think about the broader goals of the assignment: what kind of thinking is being asked for, such as deciding whether you agree with one theorist more than another, trying out a method of analysis, or learning a new skill like close reading or data analysis. Understanding the goals helps you tell whether you are on the right track. If you are unsure what to do, email your instructor or go to office hours.

## What do action verbs like analyze, compare, or discuss mean in an assignment?
Look for action verbs such as analyze, compare, discuss, explain, make an argument, propose a solution, trace, or research. They show what you are being asked to do. Unless the instructor says otherwise, most paper assignments ask you to make an argument, so even "discuss" or "consider" usually means you should offer an arguable claim. For example, if asked to discuss several proposals, you would not just list and summarize them. You would analyze them in relation to each other and make a claim, though you need to summarize them first. Likewise, if asked to compare sources, a list of similarities and differences is not enough. You are generally expected to make a claim about how the sources relate to each other.

## How do I know the scope and audience of my essay assignment?
Check the instructions for scope. Are you supposed to use sources beyond those read in class? Should your focus be narrow, such as a passage, a document, or a claim, or can you choose your own focus? If your instructor says not to use outside sources, follow that instruction, because they want your thinking about the assigned sources. Also consider your audience. In a research paper, do not assume your reader has read your sources, so explain what they say. If you are writing only about assigned sources, still give enough context for readers to follow your argument. If you are unsure how much your readers know, ask your instructor.

## What is a strong analytical question for an essay?
A strong analytical question speaks to a genuine dilemma in your sources, such as a real confusion, problem, ambiguity, or gray area where readers could have different opinions. It has an answer that is not obvious. "What did the author say?" is not strong, because every reader would answer it the same way, while a question about reconciling two points in a text is stronger. It is complex enough to need a whole essay and elicits reflection and argument rather than summary. It can also be explored using the sources you have, not generalizations or outside research. Your answer to the question becomes your thesis.

## How do I come up with an analytical question?
Look for points of tension in your sources, either within one source or among several. These are moments where you need to stop and think. Examples include a published view that doesn't convince you, an inconsistency, gap, or ambiguity in the evidence, an unexpected wrinkle, a conclusion that doesn't add up, a controversy that needs resolving, an ignored problem, or a piece of evidence that deserves a closer look. The path is point of tension, then analytical question, then thesis. Tips: "how" and "why" questions usually require more analysis than "who," "what," "when," or "where" questions. Good questions can highlight patterns, connections, contradictions, or implications, and they set the scope of your argument.

## What makes a strong thesis statement?
Your thesis is the central claim of your essay, your main insight about your source or topic. It should appear early and be followed by a logically built argument. A strong thesis is arguable, meaning a thoughtful reader could disagree and would need your analysis of the evidence to see how you reached your claim. It usually answers an analytical question or proposes a solution to a problem you have framed, and framing it tells readers what is at stake and why they should care. A strong thesis is arguable rather than descriptive, and it is the right scope: too narrow and you can't explore the topic in depth, too broad and you can't support it with the available sources.

## What is the difference between an analytical claim and a normative claim?
Many assignments ask for analytical claims, which are based on interpretation of facts, data, or sources. Some assignments ask for normative claims, which are claims of value or evaluation about how things should be rather than how they are. A normative claim argues for the importance of something, an action that should be taken, or the way the world should be. You make normative claims when you write a policy memo, a proposal, or an essay based on your own opinion. Make sure you understand which type of claim your assignment is asking for. A normative thesis tells readers what to do about an issue, but it does not tell them how to understand the source itself.

## How can I tell if my thesis is too descriptive, too broad, or too obvious?
A descriptive thesis only summarizes points a reader could already see, so it isn't arguable and readers don't need your essay. A broad thesis is arguable but vague. It doesn't say which issues it focuses on or why they matter, so readers and the writer don't know what to focus on. A strong thesis makes a specific claim that can be supported with evidence from the text. Ask yourself:
- Is it truly arguable, or would most readers automatically agree?
- Is it too obvious?
- Is it complex enough to need a whole essay?
- Can it be supported with evidence from the text rather than generalizations or outside research?
- Would anyone want to read a paper developing it?

## How do I write a good essay introduction?
An introduction generally presents an analytical question or problem and then gives your answer, the thesis. You don't need a dramatic hook. Instead, explain why your essay will be interesting by framing the question or problem and showing why it matters. A strong introduction includes three common elements: orienting information, an explanation of what's at stake, and your thesis. Orienting information gives readers enough context to understand and appreciate your thesis, without summarizing sources in detail. What's at stake means explaining why anyone needs to read an essay arguing this thesis, such as whether it challenges an accepted view, offers a new way to consider a concept, or puts something in a surprising context. Saying the topic matters in general is not enough.

## What should I avoid in an essay introduction?
Avoid a very general opening sentence about all of human history, because it won't explain what is interesting about your topic. Avoid a "funnel" introduction that starts with a broad statement and narrows down, since broad generalizations don't add to readers' understanding of your specific topic. Avoid beginning with a dictionary definition. If a concept is complicated, define it in detail later, and if it isn't, readers already know it. Avoid giving too much detail that readers could better understand later in the paper. Conventions vary by discipline. Some courses accept a two-paragraph or longer introduction, while others, such as some Government courses, expect a shorter one with a preview of the argument. Ask about expectations in a new discipline.

## How do I write a strong paragraph with a topic sentence, evidence, and analysis?
Focus each paragraph on one main point and begin a new paragraph when you move to a new point or example. The number of paragraphs depends on the steps needed to build your argument. A strong paragraph usually has three elements. The topic sentence makes a claim or states a main idea and signals how the paragraph connects to your larger argument. A purely descriptive sentence doesn't tell readers what the paragraph will do. Evidence can be quotations, paraphrased material, data, results, or primary sources. Analysis tells readers what you want them to understand about the evidence and how it supports your claim. End with a sentence about what readers can now understand, not a summary or a brand-new idea.

## How do I write good transitions between sentences and paragraphs?
Your goal is to help readers see how ideas connect to each other and to the big picture. One technique is to start with old information, something familiar to the reader, and then introduce new information. Repeating a key word from the previous sentence or paragraph makes the link clear. Transitional words should be chosen to match the relationship between ideas:
- comparison or contrast: similarly, in contrast, on the other hand
- agreement, concession, or disagreement: however, nevertheless, admittedly
- cause and effect: therefore, consequently, thus (use only for real causal links)
- explanation: for example, in other words, in particular
- conclusion: ultimately, overall, as a result

If you're unsure, write a note in the margin explaining how two paragraphs relate, then pick the transition that fits.

## How do I structure and organize a longer essay?
If you're used to the five-paragraph essay, longer essays can feel daunting. Try three strategies.
- **Decompose your thesis into paragraphs:** put your thesis at the top of a page and list the main claims needed to argue it, then the sub-claims that support each one.
- **Use subheadings, even if you remove them later:** informal subheadings in an early draft help you see how your ideas fit together, and you can revise them for readers or delete them later.
- **Create a reverse outline from your draft:** list the point of each paragraph, then check whether the order makes sense, whether there are gaps, whether topic sentences state the main points, whether paragraphs repeat each other or hold too many points, and whether any points belong earlier.

## How do I write and address counterarguments in an essay?
Your readers may not agree with you, so you need to consider counterarguments, the reasonable challenges to your thesis or other claims. Think of them as alternative answers to your question, and explain why your thesis is stronger. Don't add one at the end just because you're supposed to. Keep thinking about where a thoughtful reader could disagree as you write. A counterargument might point to a problem with a conclusion, an assumption, or a key term, to evidence you haven't considered, or to a drawback, consequence, or alternative interpretation. To address one:
1. State it and explain why a reasonable reader could raise it.
2. Counter it. You may show your argument still holds, point out a flaw, concede part of it, explain why it's not relevant, or refine your own argument.
3. Choose language carefully: "but" or "however" signals refutation, while "nevertheless" or "still" signals your argument isn't weakened.

If you can't refute a counterargument, you may revise your thesis. You don't need to address every possible counterargument.

## What should I put in my essay conclusion?
There is no single right answer, and it depends on your argument and discipline. A STEM paper might raise questions for further study, while a literature paper might return to a quotation that now reads differently. Check with your instructor. General guidelines:
- **The "what":** make a transition from your last body paragraph, either a few sentences of summary or a sentence echoing your thesis. You don't need an exhaustive summary in a short paper.
- **The "so what":** remind readers of the stakes from your introduction, possibly in a broader context.
- **The "now what":** leave readers with final thoughts, such as new understanding, new questions, other applications, actions that should be taken, or a larger context.

Avoid restating everything, introducing a big counterargument you can't refute, apologizing for what you didn't cover, and fake transitions like "in conclusion" before sentences that aren't conclusions.
